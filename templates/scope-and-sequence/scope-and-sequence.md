# Scope and sequence worksheet

This is a worksheet for the people who know the language and the learners to fill in together. It does not tell you what to teach or in what order. That knowledge is yours. Its job is to make you decide the sequence deliberately and write it down, so every unit and lesson can be built on it.

Fill it in as a group where you can. Leave things blank if you don't know yet. A gap is information too.

## Part 4. Levels, defined by your material

The number of levels, and what learners can do at each, depends on what you have. A rich archive may support short texts of a few hundred words. A language with little documentation may stop at single words and short phrases, and that is a real and valuable level.

The Chinuk Wawa program that inspired this method reached short essays of about 250–500 words, but that is one program with extensive material and many years of development. It is an example, not a target.

## Part 5. Deciding what to teach first

Answer these before filling in the sequence.

1. What do learners most need to say or understand in the first week? 
2. Which words can be tied directly to real objects, people or places around learners? 
3. Which sentence patterns appear most often in the speech and recordings you have? 
4. Which patterns depend on others? (If A is needed to understand B, A comes first.) 
5. Which sounds are hardest for learners? When should they meet them? 
6. What does the community want learners to be able to say, and why? (For example greetings, ceremony, family, work, land.) 
7. What should *not* be taught yet, or to whom?

Record dependencies in `grammar.csv` under `prerequisite_ids`. The checker uses them to catch a lesson that teaches a pattern before its prerequisite.
